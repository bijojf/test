5.1  Understanding of Requirements & Proposed Approach 

Weighting: 20%  |  Word limit: 1500  |  Draft length: ~1,290 words 

Staffordshire's established SME base faces a specific and well-evidenced gap: businesses that have survived the start-up phase and are trading successfully after five or more years, but which lack the structured support, leadership capacity, and digital/AI capability needed to move from stability into deliberate, sustained growth. Generic business support tends to be aimed either at start-ups or at large corporates; this programme sits deliberately in between, targeting established, growth-ready businesses across advanced manufacturing, logistics, construction, agri-tech, health and social care, and rural enterprise. 

Our proposed delivery model is built around the three-stage structure set out in the Specification, translated into a practical 12-week cohort journey for up to 10 businesses per cohort, repeated across six cohorts over the two-year pilot. 

Stage 1 — Diagnostics and Growth Planning (Weeks 1–3) 

Each business is allocated a named mentor at the point of onboarding, who leads a structured diagnostic combining a financial and operational review, a leadership and capability assessment, and an AI/digital-readiness scan (see Section 5.3 and 5.5). The output is a single-page baseline profile per business, scored against consistent metrics (turnover, productivity proxy, leadership capacity, digital maturity) so that progress is measurable at exit. From this diagnostic, the mentor and business owner co-produce an individual growth plan: 3–5 prioritised actions across finance, people, systems and AI adoption, each with an owner, timeframe and success measure. Producing the plan collaboratively — rather than handing over a generic template — is what makes it usable; owners of established SMEs are typically time-poor and will only act on plans they helped shape. 

Stage 2 — Resourcing Growth (Weeks 4–8) 

With the growth plan agreed, Stage 2 resources delivery against each priority. Cohort-wide workshops cover common themes (financial clarity and forecasting, leadership and delegation, systems and process improvement, and practical AI adoption), while 1:1 mentoring time is used to apply these themes to each business's specific plan. Where a business's plan surfaces a need outside our core team's expertise — for example, a specific funding application or legal structuring question — we broker access to the relevant specialist adviser or existing Growth Hub service rather than duplicating provision that already exists locally. 

Sector tailoring happens at two levels. First, cohort composition and workshop content are adjusted so that, for example, a cohort weighted toward advanced manufacturing spends proportionately more time on productivity and automation, while a cohort with more health and social care or rural businesses spends more time on workforce capacity and digital inclusion. Second, 1:1 mentoring is always sector-contextualised: the mentor works through each business's specific market and supply chain, not a generic case study. 

Rural engagement is treated as a design constraint, not an afterthought. We recognise that rural businesses face practical barriers to a 12-week programme — travel time, connectivity, and lower awareness of Growth Hub provision. Our blended delivery model defaults to virtual delivery for standard mentoring and workshop sessions, with in-person contact concentrated at the diagnostic (Stage 1) and milestone review (start/end of Stage 2 and Stage 3) points, held at accessible venues across the county rather than a single central location. Where connectivity is a genuine barrier we will work with the mentor to schedule around it rather than treat non-attendance as disengagement. 

Stage 3 — Delivery and Accountability (Weeks 9–12, plus follow-up) 

Stage 3 shifts from planning to accountability. Businesses report progress against their growth plan KPIs at a structured checkpoint, supported by their mentor; where an action has stalled, the mentor works with the business to problem-solve rather than simply record non-delivery. Each cohort closes with a showcase/exit review attended by the Steering Group and Operational Delivery Group, at which businesses present their growth plan progress and updated diagnostic score. 

Outcomes are designed to continue well beyond the 12-week programme. Every business exits with: a live growth plan owned by them (not a report that sits on a shelf); a relationship with their mentor available for a defined period of light-touch follow-up; and, where relevant, a warm handover into other Growth Hub or Staffordshire Chambers provision for continuing support. We will also establish an alumni network across cohorts so that businesses that have completed the programme can continue peer learning with each other and with future cohorts, extending the value of the two-year pilot beyond its formal end date. 

Governance and fit with SCC/SSGH oversight 

We will report into the Steering Group and Operational Delivery Group as set out in the Specification, providing baseline and progress data in a format the Authority specifies, and will flag delivery risks (participant readiness, rural engagement, duplication with existing provision) proactively rather than at scheduled reporting points only.

5.2  Cohort Recruitment and Selection 

Weighting: 15%  |  Word limit: 750  |  Draft length: ~640 words 

Recruitment will be run as a joint effort with SCC and the Stoke-on-Trent & Staffordshire Growth Hub (SSGH), using their existing channels and networks rather than competing with them. We will promote the programme through the Growth Hub's own communications, the Staffordshire Business and Enterprise Network (SBEN), Staffordshire Chambers of Commerce forums, and direct outreach to businesses already known to SCC/SSGH as scale-up candidates. We will also use targeted digital promotion (LinkedIn and sector-specific channels) and short explainer content aimed at owner-managers who may not proactively search for business support. 

Eligibility is assessed against the Specification's criteria in two steps. An initial screening confirms the hard eligibility criteria — trading 5+ years, based in Staffordshire, and within or adjacent to the priority sectors — via a short expression-of-interest form. Businesses that pass screening move to a growth diagnostic call (30–45 minutes) with a named mentor, covering current turnover and trajectory, leadership structure, existing use of technology/AI, and the specific growth barriers the business faces. 

The diagnostic call produces a readiness score across four weighted dimensions: growth ambition and commitment (does the owner want to scale, and can they commit the time); operational readiness (is the business stable enough to absorb change); leadership capacity (is there someone able to act on a growth plan); and digital/AI readiness (a starting point for the Stage 1 AI roadmap, not a barrier to entry — low current AI use is not itself disqualifying). 

Selection then balances individual readiness scores against cohort composition: we aim for a sector mix that supports meaningful workshop content and peer learning (see 5.1), rather than filling a cohort purely on a first-come basis. We will agree the final cohort list with SCC/SSGH before onboarding, ensuring transparency and a joint view of priority sectors and rural representation for each cohort. 

Onboarding for selected businesses includes: a welcome session setting expectations and the 12-week structure; agreement and signature of a participation commitment (confirming the time the owner and any nominated deputy will commit, and data-sharing consent for KPI tracking); and scheduling of the Stage 1 diagnostic and mentor allocation. We ask each business to nominate a single named lead (usually the owner or a senior deputy) accountable for engagement, reducing the risk of drop-off through diffused ownership. 

To protect commitment through the 12 weeks, we build accountability into the structure itself rather than relying on goodwill: growth plans are co-owned and reviewed at fixed checkpoints, mentors proactively flag disengagement early to the Operational Delivery Group, and the cohort's own showcase/exit review creates peer pressure to deliver. Where a business genuinely cannot continue, we will agree a managed exit with SCC/SSGH and consider a waitlisted replacement for future cohorts. 

5.3  Supporting Growth Potential 

Weighting: 15%  |  Word limit: 1000  |  Draft length: ~870 words 

The value of this programme rests on each business leaving with a growth plan that is genuinely theirs — specific, prioritised, and actionable — rather than a generic template. Our approach to producing this, and to embedding a tailored AI adoption roadmap within it, has three elements: structured diagnosis, trusted advisory input, and sustained accountability through 1:1 and peer mechanisms. 

Building the bespoke growth plan and AI adoption roadmap 

The Stage 1 diagnostic (Section 5.1) identifies each business's specific growth constraints and opportunities across finance, operations, people and technology. The mentor then works with the owner to translate this into no more than 3–5 prioritised initiatives, deliberately limited so that a time-poor SME owner can realistically deliver them within the programme window and beyond. The AI adoption roadmap is built as an integral part of this plan, not a bolt-on: for each business we identify one or two specific, low-friction AI use cases matched to their actual operations (for example, using AI tools for quoting, scheduling, customer communications, or management reporting) rather than presenting a generic list of tools. We prioritise adoption that a business can sustain itself after the programme ends, over more ambitious use cases that would require ongoing external support. 

The role of trusted advisers 

Where a growth plan surfaces a decision needing specialist input — for example, a significant investment, a financing decision, or a structural/legal question — the mentor brings in the right expertise rather than attempting to cover it themselves. This may mean drawing on our own wider associate network, or brokering a warm introduction to existing Growth Hub, Chambers, or accountancy/legal partners already trusted by the business community in Staffordshire. The mentor's role here is as a trusted, consistent point of contact who curates the right input at the right time, so the business owner is not left navigating a fragmented support landscape alone. 

1:1 strategy checkpoints 

Each business receives a minimum of four structured 1:1 checkpoints across the 12 weeks (in addition to ad hoc mentor contact): an initial plan-setting session at the end of Stage 1, a mid-point progress and problem-solving session in Stage 2, a pre-exit review in Stage 3, and a light-touch follow-up session after programme completion. Checkpoints are deliberately structured around the business's own growth plan KPIs, so they function as genuine strategic accountability sessions rather than generic catch-ups. 

Peer-to-peer learning and collaboration 

Cohort workshops (Section 5.1) are designed to create structured peer exchange, not just one-way content delivery: businesses share what they are trying and what is and is not working, facilitated so that more experienced or further-along participants support others without the sessions being dominated by a single voice. We will also establish informal peer 'buddy' pairings within each cohort where sector or challenge overlap makes this valuable, and the cross-cohort alumni network (Section 5.1) extends this peer support beyond the 12-week window, allowing later cohorts to learn from the practical experience of earlier ones. 

Taken together, this approach ensures growth potential is supported not through a one-off diagnostic report, but through a sustained cycle of tailored planning, expert input where genuinely needed, and structured accountability — which is what converts a growth plan into delivered growth.

5.5  Use of AI & Proposed Approach 

Weighting: 10%  |  Word limit: 500  |  Draft length: ~440 words 

AI adoption is treated as a practical, business-led theme throughout delivery, not a separate module. Growth and AI readiness are assessed together at Stage 1 diagnostic: alongside financial and leadership questions, we assess each business's current digital tooling, staff confidence with technology, and the specific operational tasks (quoting, scheduling, reporting, customer service, marketing content) where AI could realistically reduce cost or free up owner/staff time. This produces a simple readiness rating used to calibrate how ambitious each business's AI roadmap (Section 5.3) can sensibly be. 

Our own delivery draws directly on tools we use day to day, giving businesses a credible, hands-on demonstration rather than a theoretical overview: ChatGPT and Microsoft Copilot for drafting, summarising and report generation; Claude for more structured analysis and document work; Google's AI Studio/Gemini tooling for experimentation with workflow automation; and Canva's AI features for fast, low-cost marketing and communications content — all chosen because they are low-cost or free at entry level, meaning what we demonstrate is genuinely something a small business can adopt itself without ongoing specialist support. 

We are conscious of the risk flagged elsewhere in this procurement around AI 'hallucination' and misleading outputs, and we apply the same discipline to businesses we support as we do to our own tender preparation: AI is used to accelerate drafting and analysis, but outputs are always checked and verified by the mentor and business owner before being relied on, particularly for anything financial or contractual. 

Impact is measured at business level (movement in the digital/AI readiness score between Stage 1 diagnostic and Stage 3 exit, and confirmation of at least one adopted AI-enabled process per business) and at programme level (aggregate adoption rate across each cohort, reported to the Operational Delivery Group alongside the other KPIs in Section 5.7), so that AI adoption is evidenced rather than simply claimed.

5.7  Monitoring, Reporting & Performance Management 

Weighting: 5%  |  Word limit: 750  |  Draft length: ~620 words 

Every business is baselined at the Stage 1 diagnostic against a consistent set of metrics: turnover and a simple productivity proxy (e.g. turnover per employee), a leadership/capability score, and a digital/AI readiness score (Section 5.5). This baseline is agreed with the business owner and forms the reference point against which all subsequent progress is measured — for the individual business, for the cohort, and in aggregate across the two-year contract. 

At programme level, we will track KPIs aligned to the outcomes set out in the Specification: increased turnover; productivity improvement; job creation; leadership capability improvement; and AI adoption (proportion of businesses that have adopted at least one new AI-enabled process by exit). We will agree the precise KPI set and reporting format with SCC/SSGH at mobilisation, so that our reporting matches whatever the Authority's own outcomes framework and Steering Group reporting cycle requires, rather than us imposing our own format. 

Tracking through each cohort is built into the 1:1 checkpoint structure described in Section 5.3: at each checkpoint, the mentor records progress against the individual business's growth plan actions and updates the relevant metric where it has moved (for example, a completed AI adoption, a leadership action taken, an early revenue impact). This gives us live, checkpoint-by-checkpoint tracking data rather than relying solely on a single before/after comparison, which lets us identify and escalate disengagement or stalled progress to the Operational Delivery Group early, rather than only at cohort exit. 

At cohort exit, we re-baseline each business against the same metrics used at Stage 1, producing a clear before/after comparison per business and, aggregated, per cohort. Where a metric such as turnover uplift will only be evident after the 12-week window (given normal reporting/accounting cycles), we will agree with SCC/SSGH a light-touch follow-up data point (for example, at 6 and 12 months post-cohort) so that outcomes genuinely attributable to the programme are captured, rather than only what is visible within the delivery window itself. 

We will provide regular written reporting to the Operational Delivery Group (covering cohort recruitment, delivery progress, KPI tracking and any risks) and support the Steering Group's strategic oversight with periodic summary reporting across cohorts, in whatever format and frequency the Authority specifies at mobilisation. All participant-level data will be handled in line with GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018, with clear consent obtained from each business at onboarding for the data sharing required to support KPI tracking and reporting. 

5.8  Programme Additionality 

Weighting: 5%  |  Word limit: 750  |  Draft length: ~640 words 

Staffordshire's economy has been through substantial restructuring over recent decades — it was among the hardest-hit areas nationally during the early-2010s financial crisis but has since shown considerable resilience, recovering strongly through the pandemic and diversifying beyond its traditional manufacturing base into advanced manufacturing, logistics, construction, agri-tech, health and social care, and a growing rural and green economy. That resilience has been built on exactly the kind of established, mid-sized businesses this programme targets: firms old enough to have weathered structural change, but not yet operating at the scale or pace their owners want. 

We recognise the county's current priorities are explicitly aligned with this programme's brief. Staffordshire County Council has set out an ambition around AI-enabled productivity for existing SMEs — including a proposed Virtual AI Skills Academy — with a clear emphasis on practical adoption for smaller and start-up businesses rather than large-scale infrastructure investment, and identifying the same priority sectors named in the Specification. Our AI adoption approach (Section 5.5) is designed to complement this ambition directly: businesses leaving our programme with at least one adopted AI-enabled process are a practical, evidenced contribution to that county-wide goal, and we would welcome the opportunity to align our cohort AI content with any Virtual AI Skills Academy provision as it develops, to avoid duplication and instead reinforce it. 

We are also conscious that Staffordshire's established SME and business-support landscape is already well served by trusted local bodies — the Growth Hub itself, Staffordshire Chambers of Commerce, the Staffordshire Business and Enterprise Network (SBEN), and district-level business hubs and grant schemes. Our approach deliberately works through and alongside these bodies rather than around them: for recruitment (Section 5.2), for brokering specialist input a business's growth plan may need (Section 5.3), and for warm handover at programme exit (Section 5.1), so that the accelerator adds a genuinely new layer of intensive, sustained 1:1 support rather than duplicating provision that already exists. 

Rural engagement is a specific and recognised challenge in Staffordshire, where distance from the county's larger centres and, in places, weaker connectivity can leave rural businesses under-represented in county-wide programmes. Our blended delivery model (Section 5.1) is built around this reality rather than assuming rural businesses can engage on the same terms as those based centrally.
